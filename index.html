03 Why this actually works

The problem with a simple substitution

If you just swap each letter for one fixed symbol (A→△, B→□, …), you've made a puzzle, not a secure cipher. English text has a very predictable letter-frequency fingerprint — E shows up roughly 12% of the time, T and A close behind, while J, Q, X, and Z barely appear at all. Count how often each symbol shows up in the ciphertext, match the most common one to E, and the whole message unravels in minutes.

The homophonic fix

The trick — the one behind the Zodiac's ciphers — is to give common letters several interchangeable symbols instead of one, and rare letters just a single symbol. Below, E is assigned – different glyphs and Z gets only –. Every time you type an E, the encoder randomly picks one of its glyphs. That spreads E's true frequency out across several symbols, so no single symbol stands out anymore — frequency analysis on symbols alone stops working.

The real Zodiac ciphers

In 1969, the Zodiac Killer mailed newspapers a 408-symbol cryptogram (now called Z408) split across three letters, split into pieces, split into a 63-symbol homophonic substitution alphabet very similar in spirit to what this page just built for you. It was cracked within a week by a schoolteacher, Donald Harden, and his wife Bettye — largely by guessing the word "KILL" would appear and working outward from there. A second, harder cipher (Z340) resisted every attempt for 51 years until a team of independent codebreakers — David Oranchak, Sam Blake, and Jarl Van Eycke — cracked it in December 2020 using custom software that tested thousands of symbol-transposition patterns automatically. A third cipher, Z13, remains unsolved to this day.

04 The bigger picture: how ciphers evolve

Caesar cipher

Shift every letter by a fixed amount (A→D, B→E, …). Trivial to break — there are only 25 possible shifts to try.

Monoalphabetic substitution

Any fixed letter-to-symbol mapping. Harder — millions of possible keys — but frequency analysis cracks it almost every time, as shown above.

Homophonic substitution

What this page does. Multiple symbols per letter, weighted by frequency, flattens the statistical fingerprint. Much stronger, but patterns in word length, symbol repetition, and context can still leak information.

Polyalphabetic (Vigenère)

Uses a repeating keyword to shift letters differently depending on position, so the same plaintext letter maps to different ciphertext letters throughout — considered unbreakable for centuries until Kasiski and Babbage found ways to recover the keyword length.

Modern cryptography

Today's encryption (AES, RSA, elliptic-curve) doesn't hide letter patterns by hand — it relies on mathematical problems (like factoring huge numbers) that are computationally infeasible to reverse without the key, even with every computer on Earth.
